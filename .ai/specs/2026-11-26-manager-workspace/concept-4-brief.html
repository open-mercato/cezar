Brief

A short note from a chief of staff, rewritten after each check: what needs you (three points at most, one button each), what is fine, and what it took care of. Read it like a message; act inline.

DeviceDayPress ? in the prototype for keys

The four flows, calm by default

  • Something needs me. Point 1 carries the primary button; 2 and 3 have plain buttons. Anything beyond three waits for the next brief.
  • A goal. Every goal named in the note is a link to its page and its work.
  • Something recurring. Reply in plain words (“every Monday, update dependencies”); the next brief confirms the new rule. Or How it works.
  • Pause or adjust. How it works under the note, or reply “pause until tomorrow”.

Power, one or two steps away

  • ⌘K (or the search icon on a phone) finds any goal, task, pull request, automation or command. Type “nightly”, pick Nightly QA, and its switch, Run now and Edit are right there.
  • Everything (key E, or Tasks in the phone bar) lists goals, tasks, pull requests, recurring work and rules as plain tables, with a switch on every recurring row.
  • Keys: ↵/Y main action, N not now, 1–3 answers, M more, R rules, C/T chat and back, Esc back, ? all keys.

The three-second test

The eye lands on the headline sentence, but acting means reading a paragraph first. Closer to ten seconds than three: partial.

Good

  • The calmest way to catch up after a meeting or a day away.
  • Explains in context, the way a colleague would.
  • Reads well on a phone, and can be the morning summary too.

Not good

  • Every rewrite is a model call; skip it and the note goes stale.
  • Prose hides structure: hard to find one PR or count twenty tasks.
  • More than three decisions do not fit, by design.

The chat and Now

The note replaces the manager’s routine posts. As Now, a cross-manager brief is appealing but costly; better as an optional morning note than as the inbox.
